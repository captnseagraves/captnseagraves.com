## Oil, god, and open weights

The most interesting fight in AI right now is about metaphors. On July
24 a coalition led by Nvidia and Microsoft published
["Open Weights and American AI Leadership"](https://images.nvidia.com/pdf/Open-Weights-and-American-AI-Leadership.pdf),
arguing that open-weight models should be supported, not restricted —
that diffusing the technology across every American business beats
concentrating it in a couple of frontier labs. Anthropic answered with
[its own position](https://www.anthropic.com/news/position-open-weights-models)
on July 27. And Packy McCormick's
["AI is Oil, Not God"](https://www.notboring.co/p/ai-is-oil-not-god)
framed the stakes in one line: if AI is god — a potential
superintelligence — lockdown logic makes sense; if AI is oil — a
powerful input to everything — you want it commodified, competitive,
and everywhere.

I come to this fight with priors. Open models versus closed models is
open-source versus closed-source software, and I've spent my career on
the open side of that argument — most of it in crypto, which is
open-source software with money at stake. What that decade taught me:
open is slower, and open wins. A system that outside parties can
review, fork, and build on ends up stronger and more resilient than
anything one business group develops behind a wall. So when the two
leading closed-frontier labs tell me open weights are dangerous, I
notice the argument and I also notice who's making it. It is genuinely
reasonable to fear what a hostile state or a garage bioterrorist does
with a capable open model. It is also convenient that the fear points
toward a world where the safe AI is the kind only they sell.

But here's what surprised me when I steelmanned it: Anthropic's written
position is closer to mine than the discourse suggests. They explicitly
deny wanting bans — they want safety testing for all sufficiently
capable models, open or closed, plus chip export controls and a
crackdown on state-backed distillation. My position is open-weight
models with real safeguards. On paper, those two views nearly touch.
The actual fight is narrative and incentives — who gets to define
"safe," and whose business model the definition happens to protect.

And I'll name the hard problem instead of pretending to resolve it —
the same one Matt Kaufman of Collaborative Fund raises in the exchange
McCormick shares: once weights are public, fine-tuning can strip the
safeguards right back out. "Open with safeguards" is partly an
unsolved engineering and governance problem, and anyone who tells you
it's settled — in either direction — is selling something.

What tips me toward open anyway is the nation-state math. Chinese labs
shipped models this month that are close behind the American frontier.
If the United States regulates open weights into a corner, the
open-weight layer of the world's AI stack gets built anyway — just
somewhere else, on someone else's values, and we inherit the
proliferation without the leadership. Leading the frontier isn't
enough; someone is going to lead the fast-follow layer too, and I'd
rather it be us.

So: oil or god? Honestly, a little of both. It's not conscious, so
it's not god. But it's already steering decisions at a societal scale
most people can't see, and "just a tool" undersells the thing's edge —
enormous benefit and real harm balanced on a knife. Fair warning on
sourcing: McCormick's full answer to Kaufman sits behind his paywall,
so I won't claim to know where he lands. Here's where I do: prefer the
open ecosystem, stay honestly afraid of the bio and cyber tail risks,
and insist the US shape how this diffuses rather than outsource that
to whoever builds it next.

## What I shipped

Thirty commits at [Ubitel](https://ubitel.com), most of them the
unglamorous middle of consumer-app development: making the contact and
messaging layer trustworthy. Contact photos now persist and sync across
devices instead of vanishing on refresh. Settings survive a server sync
instead of being silently overwritten — each section keeps its own
last-write-wins timestamp. Messaging key bundles stopped quietly
evicting themselves, which had been quietly breaking message delivery.
Group chat came out of a rough merge stabilized; onboarding got
referral codes that auto-redeem; checkout got a clean fallback when a
wallet can't cover a plan; the backend got an in-app user directory
with a privacy opt-out, and account deletion that actually erases the
messaging account behind it. Then a 63-screen QA checklist to keep it
all honest.

On my own stack: this site finally has a
[/writing](https://captnseagraves.com/writing) section, and I built the
pipeline that produced this post — a Claude Code skill that collects
every commit across my dev folder, digests my working sessions and
conversations, interviews me, and drafts the essay for me to edit. The
rule I care most about is baked in: it is not allowed to invent an
accomplishment. Where it lacks evidence, it leaves a blank and asks.

## What I've been thinking about

Ownership. I spent two long sessions this week in structured interviews
about my own operating system — one on how I work, one on what I
actually want. The ten-year picture that holds up under questioning
ultimately results in ownership: a cash-flowing business, work I
control, a life rooted in a single place. The crypto-to-now line above
isn't an accident; betting on open, owned, resilient systems is the
same instinct pointed at my own life.

The acquisition search ran on that thesis all week — including a pass
that mattered. I'd drafted a letter of intent on an appealing business
in Boulder, then watched the process turn frothy: buyers bidding over
my valuation, multiple LOIs in play, and the owner responding by
re-listing at $2M — up from $1.2M, on books I read as supporting more
like $800K. I didn't submit. Walking away from a deal because the
pricing stopped making sense — with FOMO pulling the other way — is
the discipline this search runs on. Meanwhile the routine work
continued: unit economics on a water-distribution route business
(landed cost per bottle, route density, what an account is actually
worth), buyer-qualification questions answered on a new listing, and
the daily broker-digest review. Employee-ownership deal structures
also stay on the board — a version of ownership I find values-aligned,
and one that could get me into a bigger deal.

## What I learned

Reliability work compounds trust in a way features don't. Nobody asks
for "per-section sync timestamps," but everyone notices when the toggle
they set is still set tomorrow. Most of what made the app feel better
this week was deleting ways it could lie to you.

Two more, from opposite ends of life: I've been deep in XMPP identity
patterns and XEP standards for the messaging work — nerdy, but it's
the plumbing under every "it just works" moment. And I finished the
back half of *Fight Right* by John and Julie Gottman — the seven
conversations. How you enter a hard conversation determines how it
ends.

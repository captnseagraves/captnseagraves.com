Most searchers aren't thinking about employee ownership at all. I wasn't either. What changed my mind wasn't ideology — it was a reunion. An old friend from college, someone I'd been through a two-year leadership program with built around social justice and environmental sustainability, had gone on to work on exactly this problem: he'd helped start [Small Capital](https://smallcapital.org), a Durango syndicate building a way for searchers to access larger pools of capital — on the condition that employee ownership is part of the deal.

That framing got my attention, because it isn't charity. It's a better deal, for every seat at the table.

## Why it works for everyone

Run the logic from each chair:

**The searcher.** With an employee-ownership investor filling the equity gap, I can access more capital than I could alone — which means a bigger deal, more cash flow, and better odds. And I'm often looking at businesses in industries where I've never operated. Walking in as "your new CEO who's never made a sandwich or fixed a furnace" is a weak pitch. Walking in with "you're all becoming part-owners of this company" is a fundamentally different one — especially in a high-skill trade where the team's buy-in *is* the business.

**The employees.** Key employees become part-owners through a trust. They get a growing share of the cash flow — real money, on top of wages — and a durable ownership stake. People who own a piece of the outcome work like it, which loops back into the searcher's odds of success.

**The seller.** Owners who spent 25 years building something care about legacy. They want the people who built it with them taken care of. A buyer who arrives with employee ownership in the structure is offering exactly that — and it shows up in how sellers pick between offers.

Almost nobody in the searcher world is running this playbook. That's the gap. The interesting question isn't whether employee ownership is good — it's whether we can build a legal structure that makes all of the above work inside the financing system that actually closes small business deals.

## The model being built in Durango

Small Capital's model, concretely: in an acquisition — say a $2M deal with a $500K equity gap — they fill the gap *on behalf of an employee-ownership vehicle*, alongside debt from lenders who want to back employee ownership. They target a 2x return, and as they're repaid from free cash flow, their ownership redeems into an Employee Ownership Trust. The investors get paid; the employees end up owning a real piece of the company they run.

Two things I respect about how they're building it. First, honesty about where they are: roughly three years validating the model and building infrastructure, still pre-first-transaction — which is what inventing a new deal structure actually looks like. Second, an integrity threshold: if the employees don't each end up meaningfully better off — their bar is every employee at least ~10% better off even assuming flat growth — the deal isn't worth doing. Employee ownership that's nominal has no teeth.

## The vehicle: an Employee Ownership Trust

The structure that fits this best, and the one I'm focused on, is the **Employee Ownership Trust**: a perpetual trust owns part of the company, and employees — as beneficiaries — receive profit distributions according to the trust's waterfall. It works in every state. Its superpower is durable governance: the same pattern Patagonia used to lock its purpose in permanently.

Two design details matter for acquisitions. In the version I'm working toward, employees get the economics — profit share and a growing ownership stake — while voting control stays with the trust's governance structure, so the buyer can still run the company. And published setup costs run roughly $20K–$100K of bespoke legal work, which is itself part of the problem: a structure this useful shouldn't cost a five-figure legal project every time. Making EOT formation close to turnkey — standard documents, standard waterfall, a few thousand dollars instead of fifty — is a buildable product, and somebody should build it.

## The day-one problem

Here's the friction: the SBA 7(a) loan is the tried-and-true engine of small business acquisition — and its underwriting currently makes day-one employee ownership very hard. Anyone owning 20% or more must personally guarantee the loan, and in practice lenders tend to pull employee-owners into that calculation even below the line. Employees who aren't writing checks get treated like borrowers. The complexity explodes, and lenders default to "this is new and weird."

It isn't for lack of trying. Congress passed the [Main Street Employee Ownership Act in 2018](https://smlr.rutgers.edu/sites/smlr/files/Documents/Faculty-Staff-Docs/3-21-18_main_street_employee_ownership_act_summary_5_copy.pdf) specifically to open SBA lending to employee-ownership transitions. Implementation didn't deliver — the groups tracking it found the follow-on guidance so restrictive that [only a trickle of loans ever happened](https://www.nceo.org/employee-ownership-blog/sba-says-main-street-employee-ownership-act-has-not-produced-many-esop-loans).

I want to be precise about the framing, because it matters: this is not "SBA is broken" or "SBA is incompatible with employee ownership." It's a structural mismatch that nobody has fully engineered around yet. Engineering around it is the actual work.

## The structure I'm building toward

My plan is to button up the whole deal exactly as it would actually happen, and present it in a form that meets every SBA requirement on its face. One of the SBA lenders I work with has already told me he'd be open to considering a structure like this — provided the employee-ownership vehicle stays under the 20% personal-guarantee line.

So the bundle looks like: the trust holds **19.9%**, below the guarantee threshold. The deal has to be big enough that giving up a fifth of the economics still makes sense for the buyer — which the extra capital is what makes possible. Each employee's share of the cash flow has to be meaningful — my working bar is the equivalent of roughly a 10% raise. And the mechanics have to survive contact with reality: vesting schedules so employees earn into the cash-flow rights over time, and clean answers for what happens when someone leaves.

The goal is a package so airtight that I can bring it to a live deal and a live lender and the conversation is short: here's the structure, here's the math, here's the paper. The next step is exactly that — assembling the actual set of documents that executes this inside the SBA 7(a) framework, so the whole thing is a bundle a lender can underwrite, not a concept a lender has to imagine.

And for searchers willing to leave SBA entirely, there's another path worth knowing exists: 100% employee-owned purchases financed by lenders who underwrite the company's cash flow instead of demanding personal guarantees. It's real, but it changes the buyer's role — and it gives up the scale and familiarity of the 7(a) machine, which is exactly what I'm trying to keep.

## Modeling it in public

I model deals for a living now, so I built this math into my open-source deal calculator ([sbcalculator.vercel.app](https://sbcalculator.vercel.app), [repo](https://github.com/captnseagraves/sbcalculator)): a redeemable investor alongside SBA debt, the free-cash-flow waterfall, year-by-year ownership migration into the trust, employee outcome projections, and a side-by-side comparison — the same deal with and without the employee-ownership capital stack.

The headline the model shows: same buyer cash, roughly double the deal, more cash flow, and a team that owns a growing piece of the company — *if* the structure can be made as routine as a 7(a) checklist.

That's the work: not a new idea, a bankable package. Some of it is being built in Durango right now, and I'm modeling it in public while I search.

If you're a lender who'd look at a sub-20% employee-ownership structure, a searcher who wants employees on the cap table, or someone who's closed one of these — I want to compare notes. And if you want to pressure-test the math: the repo is open.
